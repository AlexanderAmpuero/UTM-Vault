### Bolter (2001) — Chapter 1: Introduction: Writing in the Late Age of Print

- **The Late Age of Print**: Defines our current era as a cultural shift where printed books remain common and prestigious, but no longer hold an undisputed monopoly on organizing and preserving knowledge.
- **Historical Shift (** **"Ceci Tuera Cela"** **)**: Cites Victor Hugo’s _Notre-Dame de Paris_ ("This will destroy that") to illustrate how digital media challenges print's dominance, just as print once displaced medieval cathedral architecture as the primary repository of knowledge.
- **Monumentality vs. Changeability**: Contrasts print’s view of text as a fixed, authoritative monument with digital text's fluid nature, which empowers readers as active co-authors and reduces the distance between author and audience.
- **Fragmented Voice & Canon**: Argues that print forged a unified rhetorical voice aimed at a mass audience to build literary canons, whereas digital hypertext fractures this single voice into individualized, non-linear reading pathways.
- **Writing Space as Mind Metaphor**: Conceptualizes each writing space (papyrus roll, codex, computer display) as a visual and material field that historically serves as a central cultural metaphor for the human mind itself.

### Bolter (2001) — Chapter 2: Writing as Technology

- **Writing as Postindustrial** **Techne**: Defines technology beyond machines to include _techne_ (an interiorized craft or method for arranging verbal ideas in visual space), framing the computer as a postindustrial writing tool that processes information rather than raw mechanical power.
- **Materiality & Social Systems**: Stresses that electronic writing remains grounded in physical culture (keyboards, monitors, economics, physical strains like RSI), defining a writing technology as the sum of technical properties and social interactions rather than pure technological determinism.
- **Economies of Writing**: Explains that every era operates under an "economy of writing" (a dynamic balance of materials, techniques, and cultural attitudes) and shows how computers reanimate past marginal tools like wax tablets, typewriters, and tape recorders.
- **Remediation**: Defines remediation as the cultural process where a newer medium replaces and refashions an older one through a dialectic of both homage and rivalry.
- **Immediacy vs. Hypermediacy**: Identifies two competing desires in digital media economies: _transparent immediacy_ (the goal for the medium to disappear so the user directly experiences reality) and _hypermediacy_ (an intense awareness of and delight in the complex interface itself).
