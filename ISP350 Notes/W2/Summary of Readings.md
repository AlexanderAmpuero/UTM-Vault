### Ferris and Hedgecock (2023)
Writing systems are human inventions that bridge spoken language and meaning through visible or tactile symbols, requiring explicit instruction to master. Over millennia, human written communication evolved from early visual precursors into complex lexigraphic systems capable of systematically encoding speech sounds, morphemes, and concepts. Understanding this evolution and the structural components of writing—such as graphic units, spelling rules, visual scripts, and physical or digital channels—helps explain the cognitive and linguistic challenges learners face when acquiring literacy in a new language.
- **Protowriting:** Visual, pictorial, or mnemonic symbol systems (like pictograms or petroglyphs) created to store information or convey narratives without being linked to the syntax or sounds of a specific spoken language.
    
- **Grapheme:** The smallest structural unit in a written language (such as a letter, character, or symbol) capable of causing a distinction in meaning or representing speech sounds, syllables, or concepts.
    
- **Orthography:** The specific set of rules and conventions by which a language uses a writing system to map its written symbols to speech, encompassing spelling and letter-to-sound correspondences.
    
- **Script:** The specific graphic layout, visual design, font, or physical typographic style of a writing system's character set (e.g., Roman, Cyrillic, or Devanagari).
    
- **Rebus:** A device or principle where a graphic symbol is used to represent the sound of a word or syllable rather than its original visual meaning, serving as a key step toward phonetic writing.
    
- **Medium:** The physical or digital tool, material, or platform used to record and transmit written text, ranging from clay and paper to electronic screens.
