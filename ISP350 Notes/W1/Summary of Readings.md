### Haas (1996)

- **Core Concept**: Introduces "The Technology Question," examining the symbiotic relationship between writing and its physical, material tools.
    
- **Inherent Technology**: Asserts that writing has never been separate from technology, as tools like clay styluses and word processors physically enable and shape text production.
    
- **Critique of Transparency**: Rejects the idea of "technological transparency," where people look past the tool to focus only on its practical or teaching outcomes.
    
- **Scope of Inquiry**: Urges researchers to study technology as an embodied, culturally bound system affecting both cognitive writing processes and broader social dynamics.
    
### Baron (2009)

- **Historical Context**: Places modern digital writing within the long history of literacy tools like the pencil, printing press, and typewriter.
    
- **Initial Resistance**: Demonstrates that nearly every new writing technology was initially met with public anxiety regarding its effect on memory and intellect.
    
- **Repurposed Tools**: Highlights that tools like pencils and computers were originally created for non-literary tasks (e.g., woodworking, math) before being adapted for writing.
    
- **Main Takeaway**: Frames digital writing as a natural stage in human technological evolution rather than a threat to literacy.
    
### Marche (2022)

- **Central Argument**: Claims generative AI language models undermine the traditional college essay as the standard measure of humanities learning.
    
- **Academic Crisis**: Notes that instantly generated AI prose challenges higher education's reliance on written assignments to prove student comprehension.
    
- **Institutional Critique**: Faults humanities departments for failing to adapt to technological shifts and for not clearly defining the value of humanistic study.
    
- **Future Outlook**: Argues that automated text generation forces universities to redefine how critical thinking and original thought are taught and evaluated.
